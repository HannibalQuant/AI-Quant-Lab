The Scientific Research Operating System, or Research OS, defines the scientific obligations of AI Quant Lab. It governs how proposed explanations become testable claims, how experiments are designed before outcomes are known, how evidence is evaluated, how findings are challenged and replicated, and how accepted or rejected results become durable organizational knowledge.

The Research OS is constitutional rather than procedural. Individual disciplines may add stricter methods, but no study may weaken its requirements without an explicit, time-bounded, reviewed exception.

## 1. Research philosophy

### 1.1 Research

Research is the disciplined production and revision of knowledge through explicit questions, falsifiable claims, controlled observation, transparent evidence, and independent challenge. Its purpose is not to confirm preferred ideas or produce attractive results. Its purpose is to reduce uncertainty about a bounded question while preserving enough information for another qualified researcher to inspect and reproduce the reasoning.

Research is successful when it clarifies what is true, false, unsupported, conditional, or still unknown. A rejected hypothesis can be more valuable than a weakly supported positive result because it closes an unproductive path and improves future questions.

### 1.2 Idea

An idea is a possible direction, analogy, mechanism, or relationship that has not yet been expressed as a researchable claim. Ideas are allowed to be incomplete and speculative. They enter the research system as candidates, not evidence.

Ideas must retain origin and context so later success does not erase how much selection occurred before the final hypothesis.

### 1.3 Observation

An observation is a bounded description of something measured or directly encountered. It states what appeared, where, when, under which measurement, and relative to what reference. It does not explain why the behavior occurred.

Observations may generate questions. They cannot become hypotheses only after the same sample is used to optimize the expected answer without disclosing the exploratory origin.

### 1.4 Question

A research question defines the uncertainty the study intends to reduce. It specifies population, conditions, comparison, outcome, horizon, and relevance. A useful question is narrow enough to test and important enough that its answer would change understanding or future decisions.

### 1.5 Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a falsifiable proposition predicting a relationship or outcome under defined conditions. It includes a mechanism or clearly states when the study is descriptive. It names competing and null hypotheses and identifies observations that would count against it.

### 1.6 Experiment

An experiment is a predeclared comparison designed to distinguish among hypotheses. It defines variables, controls, evidence, metrics, stopping, and replication before the decisive outcome is inspected.

In historical market research, the researcher may not control the market, but can still control question formulation, sampling, transformation, comparison, information timing, and access to final evaluation evidence.

### 1.7 Evidence

Evidence is a recorded result or observation used to update confidence in a hypothesis. Evidence has direction, quality, relevance, independence, uncertainty, and scope. It can support, contradict, or limit a claim.

Evidence belongs to the study even when it is unfavorable. Removing adverse results changes the question after observation and violates scientific integrity.

### 1.8 Finding

A finding is a bounded interpretation of the evidence produced by a completed study. It states what changed relative to the hypothesis, how strong and uncertain the result is, and where it applies.

A finding is not accepted knowledge merely because it is statistically favorable. It remains provisional until independent validation and required replication.

### 1.9 Theory

A theory is a coherent explanatory structure connecting multiple findings and mechanisms. It generates new hypotheses and explains why relationships should recur. A theory's scope, alternatives, and contradictions remain explicit.

### 1.10 Knowledge

Knowledge is a reviewed, versioned, contextual claim with provenance, evidence, scope, uncertainty, and lifecycle status. Research produces candidate knowledge objects. The Knowledge OS determines whether they are validated, restricted, contradicted, superseded, retired, or available to named consumers.

### 1.11 Falsifiability

Every empirical claim must remain exposed to disconfirmation. A study must state:

- what observation would contradict the claim;
- what magnitude or direction would be inconsistent with expectation;
- which market, horizon, and conditions define applicability;
- which alternative explanation could account for the same evidence;
- what evidence would cause rejection, narrowing, or suspension.

A claim that changes meaning after every adverse result is not falsifiable. Reinterpreting a failure requires a new versioned hypothesis, not an edit to the original.

### 1.12 Negative results

Negative results identify absent relationships, insufficient effects, failed mechanisms, non-reproducible findings, unusable measurements, and conditions under which a strategy or method should not be trusted.

They are valuable because they:

- reduce repeated exploration of the same dead end;
- reveal boundaries and regime dependence;
- calibrate prior beliefs and power expectations;
- expose publication and selection bias;
- improve future controls and measurements;
- provide evidence against overgeneralized theories;
- preserve an honest denominator of attempted research.

Negative results receive the same lineage, review, and archival standards as positive results.

### 1.13 Intellectual honesty

Researchers must distinguish preregistered tests from exploratory analysis, source claims from project inference, absence of evidence from evidence of absence, statistical uncertainty from economic insignificance, and favorable outcomes from sound methodology.

Research confidence must be proportional to evidence, not narrative coherence or agent authority.

## 2. Research lifecycle

### 2.1 Lifecycle overview

```text
Observation
↓
Research Question
↓
Literature Review
↓
Knowledge Retrieval
↓
Hypothesis
↓
Experimental Design
↓
Experiment
↓
Evidence Collection
↓
Statistical Evaluation
↓
Interpretation
↓
Independent Validation
↓
Replication
↓
Publication
↓
Knowledge OS
```

The lifecycle is an evidence maturity model. A study may return to an earlier state, narrow its scope, or terminate. Advancement requires declared artifacts and review; it is never inferred from a favorable result.

### 2.2 Observation

The originating observation records provenance, measurement, market, horizon, time, and whether it was noticed before or after inspecting outcomes. Exploratory observations are valid but must not be mislabeled as confirmatory predictions.

### 2.3 Research question

The question is evaluated for clarity, testability, value, novelty, feasibility, ethical and policy compliance, and relation to existing knowledge. The system identifies whether answering it would change a strategy, method, risk judgment, or knowledge claim.

### 2.4 Literature review

The literature review maps primary evidence, theory, methods, replications, contradictions, retractions, and known gaps. It avoids treating citation count as truth and separates genuinely independent evidence from repeated derivation.

The review may reveal that the question is already answered, malformed, or requires a different scope.

### 2.5 Knowledge retrieval

Current eligible knowledge, prior internal studies, failed hypotheses, decisions, definitions, and unresolved contradictions are retrieved by exact version. The researcher records which knowledge influenced question and design.

### 2.6 Hypothesis

The primary, alternative, and null hypotheses are frozen before confirmatory evidence is accessed. Exploratory studies may generate hypotheses but cannot use the same undisclosed evidence as independent confirmation.

### 2.7 Experimental design

Design declares population, sample, variables, transformations, information timing, controls, baselines, metrics, comparisons, exclusion, stopping, and replication. Design choices include reasons and known sensitivities.

### 2.8 Experiment

The experiment follows the frozen design. Deviations are recorded when they occur, with cause and impact. Material deviations change the study's status or require a new study version.

### 2.9 Evidence collection

All eligible results are preserved, including failed runs, excluded observations, null results, and adverse diagnostics. Collection records missingness, quality changes, and whether the evidence was expected or discovered.

### 2.10 Statistical evaluation

Evaluation estimates effect magnitude, uncertainty, sensitivity, power, model assumptions, multiplicity, and robustness. Statistical significance is never interpreted alone and never substitutes for economic or operational relevance.

### 2.11 Interpretation

Interpretation states what the evidence supports, contradicts, or leaves unresolved. It distinguishes existence of a relationship from mechanism, predictability from actionability, and actionability from production suitability.

### 2.12 Independent validation

An independent agent challenges data lineage, chronology, design execution, statistical methods, adverse evidence, and claims. The validator does not redesign the study to obtain a favorable answer.

### 2.13 Replication

Replication repeats the decisive claim using defined independence. A finding cannot become accepted knowledge before satisfying the applicable replication threshold.

### 2.14 Publication

Publication freezes a complete, reviewed study package. It includes negative findings, deviations, limitations, alternative explanations, and links to evidence. Publication means availability for scrutiny, not endorsement for production.

### 2.15 Knowledge OS

Published findings become candidate knowledge objects with provenance, confidence, contradictions, consumers, review frequency, and retirement conditions. Rejected findings enter the Failure Database and Knowledge OS rather than disappearing.

## 3. Research question framework

### 3.1 Question standard

A research question must be answerable through observable evidence and precise enough that two qualified researchers would agree on what is being asked. It must not contain its desired answer.

### 3.2 Scope

Scope defines market or population, instrument type, horizon, period, state, decision context, unit of observation, and explicit exclusions. Scope distinguishes the target population from the available sample.

### 3.3 Motivation

Motivation explains which uncertainty, contradiction, failure, or decision the research addresses. Curiosity is acceptable for exploration, but scarce research resources require a clear expected contribution.

### 3.4 Economic rationale

The question states why the proposed behavior could exist after competition and costs. Rationales may involve risk compensation, delayed information, institutional constraints, forced flow, liquidity provision, behavioral persistence, or market design.

An economic rationale is not proof. It determines expected conditions, competing explanations, and persistence tests.

### 3.5 Expected mechanism

The mechanism describes the causal or behavioral path connecting condition to outcome. If the research is intentionally descriptive, it states that mechanism is not being established.

### 3.6 Alternative explanations

At least one credible explanation must predict some of the same observations. The question defines evidence capable of distinguishing the preferred explanation from alternatives such as general market drift, volatility exposure, liquidity events, data construction, or chance.

### 3.7 Required evidence

Required evidence includes minimum data properties, comparisons, controls, temporal availability, and independent evaluation needed to answer the question. Evidence that would be useful but unavailable is recorded separately.

### 3.8 Failure criteria

Failure criteria state results that reject the expected behavior or render the question unanswerable. Examples include insufficient sample integrity, disappearance after realistic friction, instability across reasonable definitions, or inability to separate competing explanations.

### 3.9 Success criteria

Success criteria define what would constitute a meaningful answer, not what would guarantee a favorable result. A clear negative answer can satisfy success criteria when the design is adequate.

### 3.10 Question review

Before study authorization, review asks:

- Is the question genuinely open?
- Is it falsifiable and decision-relevant?
- Did the same evidence generate and test it?
- Are population and sample distinct?
- Are alternative explanations credible?
- Would either a positive or negative result be publishable internally?

## 4. Hypothesis framework

### 4.1 Behavior

The hypothesis specifies the conditional behavior, expected direction, magnitude concept, horizon, and comparison. It does not define success by a specific indicator or optimized rule.

### 4.2 Mechanism

The hypothesis states the proposed reason the behavior occurs and which intermediate observations should follow if the mechanism is correct. Mechanism evidence is evaluated separately from outcome evidence.

### 4.3 Expected persistence

Persistence explains why the behavior may recur despite awareness and competition. It identifies forces that may sustain or erode it and the expected pace of decay.

### 4.4 Market conditions

The hypothesis defines eligible and ineligible market states, liquidity conditions, horizons, participant contexts, and structural assumptions. Conditions must be identifiable without using future outcomes.

### 4.5 Alternative hypotheses

Alternative hypotheses offer credible competing explanations or outcome directions. They receive their own predictions and failure conditions rather than serving as rhetorical objections.

### 4.6 Null hypothesis

The null represents no meaningful conditional relationship relative to the declared baseline, after relevant uncertainty and friction. It must be defined in a way that the experiment can evaluate.

### 4.7 Failure conditions

Failure conditions cover wrong direction, insufficient magnitude, instability, event concentration, dependence on post-event information, inability to identify conditions, and contradiction of the mechanism.

### 4.8 Confidence before testing

Prior confidence is recorded before decisive evidence. It is based on mechanism plausibility, prior independent evidence, measurement quality, and relevance. Recording prior confidence prevents narrative certainty from being inflated after results.

### 4.9 Hypothesis versioning

Once confirmatory testing begins, the hypothesis is immutable. Changes create a new version linked to the original and disclose which evidence motivated the change.

## 5. Experiment design

### 5.1 Purpose

Purpose states which hypothesis distinction the experiment resolves and what decision the result could inform. An experiment without a discriminating purpose is data exploration.

### 5.2 Variables

All measured, derived, controlled, conditioning, and nuisance variables are defined with units, timing, availability, transformation, and missing-data treatment.

### 5.3 Independent variables

Independent variables represent conditions or exposures whose relationship to outcomes is tested. In observational market research they may not be experimentally assigned, so confounding and selection require explicit treatment.

### 5.4 Dependent variables

Dependent variables represent the outcomes used to evaluate hypotheses. They include horizon, calculation, censoring, path dependence, and economic interpretation.

### 5.5 Controls

Controls isolate the proposed relationship from known alternatives. They may include matched periods, state controls, market controls, timing controls, simplified rules, or confounding variables.

### 5.6 Baselines

Baselines define what the hypothesis must improve upon or differ from. Appropriate baselines may include unconditional behavior, naive rules, random timing, passive exposure, simple strategy families, or incumbent knowledge.

### 5.7 Negative controls

Negative controls should not exhibit the proposed effect if the design is valid. Examples include time-shifted information, randomized labels, destroyed temporal structure, irrelevant conditions, or markets where the mechanism should not apply.

A positive negative control is evidence of leakage, confounding, data construction, or selection.

### 5.8 Positive controls

Positive controls are relationships expected to be detectable under the study's data and method. Failure to detect them indicates insufficient sensitivity, data quality, or design adequacy.

### 5.9 Evaluation metrics

Metrics are chosen before results and include effect magnitude, uncertainty, error trade-offs, economic relevance, adverse behavior, and robustness. Multiple metrics are assigned roles so the study cannot select whichever looks most favorable afterward.

### 5.10 Stopping criteria

Stopping criteria define completion, futility, safety, data insufficiency, budget limits, and invalidation. Optional stopping based on favorable results is prohibited unless explicitly designed and adjusted.

### 5.11 Replication criteria

The design specifies which finding requires replication, what independence is necessary, which dimensions may vary, and which result would count as successful, partial, failed, or contradictory replication.

### 5.12 Preregistration

Confirmatory experiments preregister question, hypotheses, sample logic, variables, exclusions, metrics, comparisons, multiplicity treatment, stopping, and replication before decisive evidence access. Exploratory analysis is labeled and may propose later confirmatory work.

### 5.13 Design review

Independent review challenges leakage, sample construction, controls, measurement timing, power, multiplicity, and whether the experiment can distinguish its hypotheses. Approval applies only to the reviewed design version.

## 6. Research bias control

### 6.1 Confirmation bias

**Risk:** seeking, weighting, or interpreting evidence to support the preferred hypothesis.

**Controls:** competing hypotheses, preregistered failure criteria, adverse-evidence search, independent interpretation, and publication of negative results.

### 6.2 Selection bias

**Risk:** the selected sample, markets, periods, observations, or studies differ systematically from the target population.

**Controls:** population definition, selection provenance, inclusion rules fixed before outcomes, sensitivity to alternate selections, and explicit limits on generalization.

### 6.3 Survivorship bias

**Risk:** only surviving instruments, strategies, repositories, or successful experiments remain visible.

**Controls:** historical membership, delisted and failed cases, full experiment denominator, and Failure Database linkage.

### 6.4 Look-ahead bias

**Risk:** information unavailable at the decision time influences earlier classification, signal, selection, or evaluation.

**Controls:** explicit information timestamps, chronological reconstruction, delayed publication handling, and independent time-consistency review.

### 6.5 Data leakage

**Risk:** evaluation information enters training, feature construction, normalization, imputation, filtering, or model selection.

**Controls:** split-aware transformations, frozen evaluation boundaries, dependency tracing, holdout access records, and negative controls.

### 6.6 Data snooping

**Risk:** repeated search across hypotheses, samples, metrics, and definitions creates chance discoveries.

**Controls:** experiment registry, full trial denominator, multiplicity adjustment, independent confirmation, research-budget limits, and separation of exploration from confirmation.

### 6.7 Overfitting

**Risk:** a theory, model, or strategy captures sample-specific noise or interactions.

**Controls:** simpler alternatives, parameter and definition stability, out-of-sample evaluation, perturbation, cross-context replication, and complexity justification.

### 6.8 Publication bias

**Risk:** positive or novel findings are retained while null, negative, and contradictory results disappear.

**Controls:** preregistered study registry, mandatory completion status, negative-result publication, and meta-analysis using the complete study set.

### 6.9 Narrative bias

**Risk:** coherent explanation is mistaken for evidence, especially after outcomes are known.

**Controls:** prior mechanism statement, alternative mechanisms, separate evidence for existence and causality, blinded interpretation where feasible, and versioned post-hoc explanations.

### 6.10 Recency bias

**Risk:** recent market behavior or recent findings receive excessive weight.

**Controls:** multi-period context, time-stratified analysis, stable review horizons, and explicit reasons when current structure should dominate older evidence.

### 6.11 Authority bias

**Risk:** claims are accepted because of author, institution, citation count, Founder preference, or agent rank.

**Controls:** evidence-first review, source and claim separation, anonymous or blinded review when useful, independent replication, and preserved dissent.

### 6.12 Bias register

Each study maintains a bias register describing applicable biases, controls, residual exposure, detection evidence, and consequences for interpretation. An unchecked material bias prevents acceptance.

## 7. Statistical governance

### 7.1 Sample quality

Sample quality precedes sample size. The study evaluates provenance, completeness, timestamp integrity, revisions, missingness, survivorship, outliers, market coverage, venue changes, dependence, and correspondence to the target population.

Poor-quality observations do not become adequate through quantity.

### 7.2 Sample sufficiency

Sufficiency depends on effect size, variability, dependence, regime coverage, number of independent events, model flexibility, and intended conclusion. Large numbers of correlated observations may contain little independent information.

The study states which inference the sample can support and which it cannot.

### 7.3 Power analysis

Before confirmatory testing, the study estimates its ability to detect an economically relevant effect under realistic variability and dependence. When conventional power calculation is unsuitable, scenario-based detectability and minimum useful effect are stated.

An underpowered null result is inconclusive, not proof of absence. An overpowered study can detect effects too small to matter.

### 7.4 Confidence intervals

Intervals communicate plausible effect ranges under declared assumptions. The study reports interval method, coverage interpretation, dependence treatment, and practical thresholds. Point estimates without uncertainty are insufficient.

### 7.5 Robustness

Robustness asks whether the conclusion survives reasonable changes in data, definitions, samples, assumptions, costs, and methods. Robustness does not mean every variation must agree; disagreement must be explainable and bounded.

### 7.6 Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity identifies which assumptions, observations, transformations, and thresholds most influence the result. A finding whose sign or interpretation changes under small reasonable variation receives reduced confidence and narrower scope.

### 7.7 Cross-validation philosophy

Cross-validation estimates generalization only when its partitioning respects the dependence and temporal structure of the question. Random partitioning is not automatically valid for time-dependent markets. Cross-validation used repeatedly for selection becomes part of training evidence and cannot serve as untouched confirmation.

### 7.8 Out-of-sample philosophy

Out-of-sample evidence is data not used to generate, select, tune, or revise the tested hypothesis and architecture. Access is controlled and recorded. Once inspected and used for revision, a sample is no longer final out-of-sample evidence for the revised candidate.

### 7.9 Walk-forward philosophy

Walk-forward analysis evaluates repeated historical decisions using only prior information, with training and evaluation advancing through time. It studies adaptation, temporal stability, and operational realism. Fold definitions, selection rules, and aggregation are declared before results.

Failed folds remain visible. A favorable aggregate cannot erase periods of unacceptable behavior.

### 7.10 Monte Carlo philosophy

Monte Carlo analysis evaluates uncertainty and path dependence through justified perturbations of trades, outcomes, order, parameters, costs, delays, missed events, or other relevant variables. Perturbations must correspond to plausible uncertainty rather than produce reassuring distributions by construction.

Simulation supplements empirical evidence; it does not replace missing market history or validate an unsupported mechanism.

### 7.11 Stress testing philosophy

Stress testing examines adverse but plausible conditions beyond the central historical sample: volatility shifts, spread expansion, liquidity withdrawal, gaps, correlation convergence, execution delay, missing data, market closure, and structural change.

Stress tests reveal vulnerability and control needs. Passing a finite set does not establish safety against all unknown conditions.

### 7.12 Multiple testing and selection

The study records all material hypotheses, variants, metrics, and samples considered. Evaluation accounts for selection and repeated trials. The more freedom used to discover a result, the stronger the independent confirmation required.

### 7.13 Statistical versus practical significance

A statistically distinguishable effect may be too small, unstable, costly, or capacity-limited to matter. Practical interpretation considers effect size, uncertainty, friction, adverse paths, opportunity cost, and decision consequence.

## 8. Replication system

### 8.1 Replication requirement

No finding becomes accepted knowledge solely from its originating study. Replication must test the decisive claim using sufficient independence to detect hidden dependence on data, researcher, method, market, horizon, or regime.

### 8.2 Internal replication

Internal replication repeats the study within AI Quant Lab using the frozen design and independent reconstruction where possible. It verifies lineage, definitions, methods, and result reproducibility.

### 8.3 Independent replication

Independent replication is conducted by an agent or research cell not involved in original hypothesis selection, experiment execution, or interpretation. It receives the published study package and preserves any deviations.

### 8.4 Cross-market replication

Cross-market replication tests other instruments or venues where the proposed mechanism predicts similar behavior. Failure may reject generality without rejecting the original bounded finding.

### 8.5 Cross-timeframe replication

Cross-timeframe replication evaluates whether the behavior is scale-dependent, aggregation-sensitive, or consistent across horizons. Transfer is required only when the mechanism predicts it.

### 8.6 Cross-regime replication

Cross-regime replication tests eligible and ineligible states. A conditional hypothesis gains support when the effect strengthens and weakens in the preregistered directions.

### 8.7 Cross-dataset replication

Cross-dataset replication uses independently sourced or constructed data to detect provider, cleaning, adjustment, or measurement artifacts.

### 8.8 Acceptance thresholds

A finding may be accepted as bounded knowledge when:

- the original study passes independent validation;
- the decisive result is computationally reproducible;
- at least one independent replication succeeds within preregistered tolerances;
- relevant cross-context replications match the claimed scope;
- effect direction and practical interpretation remain stable;
- material contradictions are resolved or explicitly bound;
- evidence survives applicable costs, controls, and sensitivity analysis;
- all negative and failed replication evidence is included;
- the Knowledge Curator and domain reviewer approve the knowledge object.

Replication is classified as:

- **Successful:** decisive claim remains within expected direction and tolerance.
- **Partial:** some components reproduce, requiring narrower scope or confidence.
- **Failed:** decisive claim does not reproduce under adequate design.
- **Contradictory:** credible replication supports an incompatible conclusion.
- **Inconclusive:** design, sample, or power cannot resolve the claim.

Acceptance threshold scales with consequence. Production-relevant claims require stronger independence, execution realism, and replication across the conditions they are expected to face.

## 9. Research output contract

### 9.1 Required study package

Every completed, terminated, or failed study produces a research package. Incomplete studies do not disappear; their status and reason are recorded.

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Research ID | Persistent identity linking all versions, evidence, reviews, and replications |
| Question | Frozen research question, scope, motivation, and decision relevance |
| Hypothesis | Primary, alternatives, null, mechanism, prior confidence, and failure conditions |
| Experimental Design | Variables, controls, baselines, metrics, sample logic, stopping, and deviations |
| Evidence | Complete eligible results, lineage, exclusions, quality, and adverse evidence |
| Statistical Summary | Effects, intervals, power, multiplicity, sensitivity, and robustness |
| Limitations | Known constraints, residual bias, missing evidence, and scope boundaries |
| Alternative Explanations | Competing interpretations and discriminating evidence |
| Negative Findings | Null results, failed tests, rejected modules, and adverse diagnostics |
| Final Recommendation | Reject, revise, replicate, bounded finding, or candidate knowledge |
| Knowledge Objects Created | Claims, evidence, failures, definitions, methods, and lessons proposed to Knowledge OS |
| Future Research Suggestions | Unresolved questions ranked by expected information value |

### 9.2 Study status

The package states whether the study is exploratory, preregistered confirmatory, validation, replication, meta-analysis, or retrospective. A study cannot change status after results to strengthen its authority.

### 9.3 Claim discipline

Claims may not exceed the design, sample, and replication. Statistical association is not called mechanism, predictability is not called profitability, and backtest actionability is not called production readiness.

### 9.4 Publication completeness

The package includes deviations, exclusions, failed runs, stopping reason, conflicting evidence, reviewer dissent, and dependency versions. A concise summary may accompany it but cannot replace the complete record.

## 10. Failure Database

### 10.1 Purpose

The Failure Database preserves the evidence needed to prevent repeated scientific mistakes and understand the boundaries of project knowledge. Failure is represented as structured research output, not reputational damage.

### 10.2 Failed hypotheses

Records include original claim, prior confidence, decisive contradicting evidence, scope, whether mechanism or outcome failed, and conditions under which reconsideration would be legitimate.

### 10.3 Failed experiments

Records include design, failure mode, detection, affected evidence, salvageable observations, and whether the question remains answerable. Failure may arise from inadequate power, contaminated data, invalid controls, non-reproducibility, or execution of a wrong design.

### 10.4 Rejected ideas

Rejected ideas retain rationale, screening evidence, redundancy with prior work, infeasibility, policy conflict, or insufficient information value. This prevents repeated proposal under different wording.

### 10.5 Unexpected outcomes

Unexpected outcomes record preregistered expectation, observation, possible explanations, whether the result is credible, and new hypotheses. They do not rewrite the original hypothesis.

### 10.6 Near misses

Near misses are conditions that almost produced invalid evidence, unauthorized access, incorrect interpretation, or operational harm but were detected before full consequence. They reveal weak controls not visible in completed failures.

### 10.7 Contradictory evidence

Contradictions link studies, definitions, data, and contexts. They remain unresolved until evidence supports reconciliation, scope partition, supersession, or accepted uncertainty.

### 10.8 Failure object

Every failure record includes identity, research parent, category, description, detection, impact, root cause, contributing factors, evidence, affected claims, containment, correction, recurrence conditions, owner, review, and lesson candidates.

### 10.9 Failure becomes knowledge

A failure becomes future knowledge when its evidence and scope are reviewed, linked to affected objects, and converted into a bounded statement that changes future question design, method, controls, or interpretation.

The system does not generalize one failure into universal prohibition. It preserves both the failed case and the reviewed lesson.

## 11. Continuous scientific improvement

### 11.1 Knowledge updates

New studies, replications, contradictions, and failures update candidate knowledge through the Knowledge OS. Existing knowledge receives impact review and may be confirmed, narrowed, deprecated, superseded, or retired.

### 11.2 Methodology reviews

Research methods are reviewed on a defined cadence and when repeated bias, replication failure, new scientific evidence, or changed markets reveal limitations. Reviews compare expected control effectiveness with observed performance.

### 11.3 Replication campaigns

The research portfolio periodically selects influential, high-consequence, weakly independent, or aging findings for coordinated replication. Replication resources are allocated by knowledge importance and uncertainty, not novelty.

### 11.4 Quality audits

Audits sample studies for preregistration integrity, evidence completeness, chronology, reproducibility, bias controls, claim discipline, negative-result retention, review independence, and Knowledge OS linkage.

Audit findings affect agent authority, methodology, and research debt.

### 11.5 Meta-analysis

Meta-analysis combines compatible studies while preserving differences in market, period, method, dependence, and publication status. It includes negative and failed studies and tests heterogeneity. Pooled estimates do not erase context-specific contradictions.

### 11.6 Retrospectives

Retrospectives evaluate the scientific process rather than rewarding favorable outcomes. They ask whether questions were valuable, hypotheses discriminating, controls effective, uncertainty calibrated, and replications informative.

### 11.7 Research debt

Research debt is unresolved scientific obligation that weakens future work. It includes unreplicated influential findings, expired assumptions, missing negative controls, unclear definitions, undocumented exploration, unresolved contradictions, underpowered claims, incomplete failure analysis, and knowledge objects without review.

Debt is visible, owned, prioritized, and prevented from silently becoming foundational knowledge.

### 11.8 Method change

Changes to the Research OS or domain methodology require evidence, alternatives, impact analysis, transition, and governance approval. Recent success alone does not justify weakening controls.

## 12. Research governance

### 12.1 Founder

**Responsibilities:** define research purpose, ethical and legal boundaries, acceptable institutional risk, capital relevance, and irreversible policy.

**Approval authority:** constitutional changes, capital-impacting research mandates, and permanent exceptions.

**Publication authority:** approval for external disclosure where ownership, legal, strategic, or capital consequences apply.

**Retirement authority:** governing research domains or institutional policies, with preserved rationale.

### 12.2 CEO Agent

**Responsibilities:** set research portfolio priorities, budgets, sequencing, conflict resolution, and lifecycle decisions.

**Approval authority:** authorize research programs, resources, and advancement to downstream candidate states after required scientific approvals.

**Publication authority:** approve internal decision-grade publication and request external publication approval.

**Retirement authority:** close programs or retire operational relevance while preserving scientific records.

### 12.3 Research Agent

**Responsibilities:** generate questions, review literature, retrieve knowledge, propose hypotheses, conduct scoped research, and publish complete research packages.

**Approval authority:** none for its own accepted knowledge; may approve completeness of delegated exploratory work within mandate.

**Publication authority:** submit study packages for review, not self-publish as validated knowledge.

**Retirement authority:** recommend retirement or rejection with evidence.

### 12.4 Quant Strategy Architect

**Responsibilities:** translate supported behavioral research into falsifiable strategy architecture and identify required experiments.

**Approval authority:** approve that a research finding is sufficiently defined for strategy experimentation, not that it is validated or production-ready.

**Publication authority:** publish reviewed strategy research recommendations with explicit status.

**Retirement authority:** reject or recommend retirement of strategy hypotheses within research scope.

### 12.5 Knowledge Curator

**Responsibilities:** maintain research identities, relationships, confidence, contradictions, failure links, knowledge lifecycle, and lessons.

**Approval authority:** approve knowledge representation and lifecycle eligibility with domain and independent review.

**Publication authority:** admit reviewed objects to Knowledge OS layers; cannot grant production authority alone.

**Retirement authority:** deprecate, supersede, or retire knowledge objects under policy and impact review.

### 12.6 Validation Agent

**Responsibilities:** independently test design execution, statistics, robustness, bias control, claims, and replication.

**Approval authority:** pass, conditionally pass, fail, or mark incomplete within scientific validation scope.

**Publication authority:** publish immutable validation and replication reports.

**Retirement authority:** recommend restriction or retirement when validation fails or accepted findings no longer reproduce.

### 12.7 QA Agent

**Responsibilities:** verify research-contract completeness, traceability, review status, artifact integrity, policy compliance, and publication readiness.

**Approval authority:** approve conformance and release completeness; cannot approve scientific truth or risk.

**Publication authority:** permit internal release only when required artifacts and reviews are complete.

**Retirement authority:** block or withdraw publication eligibility when integrity or compliance fails.

### 12.8 Separation of authority

No research-producing agent may be the sole validator, replicator, knowledge approver, and publisher of its own finding. Scientific validation, knowledge acceptance, QA completeness, and strategic use are separate decisions.

### 12.9 Publication classes

- **Exploratory note:** generates questions; not validated knowledge.
- **Completed study:** full output contract; awaiting or including validation.
- **Validated finding:** passed independent scientific review; awaiting replication where required.
- **Replicated finding:** satisfied declared replication threshold; candidate for accepted knowledge.
- **Accepted knowledge:** admitted by Knowledge OS within bounded scope.
- **Retired or contradicted finding:** preserved historically and prohibited from current eligible use unless revalidated.

### 12.10 Governance rules

1. No Research ID, no recognized study.
2. No frozen question and hypothesis, no confirmatory claim.
3. No complete evidence denominator, no trustworthy finding.
4. No independent validation, no scientific acceptance.
5. No replication, no accepted empirical knowledge.
6. No negative-result preservation, no honest research portfolio.
7. No provenance and scope, no safe reuse.
8. No Failure Database, no self-correction.
9. No authority separation, no credible approval.
10. No retirement path, no durable knowledge governance.

### 12.11 Governing outcome

The Research OS succeeds when another qualified agent can reconstruct the question, prior belief, design, evidence, statistical reasoning, deviations, negative findings, interpretation, validation, replication, and knowledge decision without relying on private memory or favorable hindsight.

If a result cannot survive that reconstruction, it may remain an idea or observation, but it is not accepted AI Quant Lab research.
